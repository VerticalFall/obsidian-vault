> I look at what I do with Fable 5.2 in the cracks of my week and think: this would've taken five people and $200,000.

> Look at that and you might ask: Why would I need a team at all?
> 
> The chart seems to agree. On OpenAI's GDPval benchmark, AI went from beating human experts 12.4% of the time in 2024 to around 83% in 2026. That's the aggregate view of the future of work: gather a lot of data and extrapolate a trend line.
> 
> But the aggregate misses something. To see it, you have to get intimate: zoom in on one real situation, with all its messiness, and extrapolate from there.
> 
> We gave everyone at Every access to the same customer survey and the same AI models.
> 
> • I found an opportunity to sell to teams.
> • @poojary_yash saw that first-time subscribers were churning because we send too much email.
> • @dabrundage noticed that many of our readers feel lonely: they're excited about AI, but people around them think they're crazy.
> • @jackcheng turned the results into a Claymation game show.
> 
> Could you ask Claude to produce what each of them found? Maybe. But generating possibilities is cheap. Knowing which ones matter is hard.
> 
> @hammer_mt ran an experiment to predict what I'd say in Slack. When Claude got 400 attempts, it produced an answer like mine 93% of the time. But when it had to rank its own answers, mine landed in its top 10% only around 60% of the time.
> 
> Each person on a team is responsible for a different part of the world. That responsibility narrows where they look, and their perspective helps them decide what matters enough to act on. A team explores more possibilities than a single default prompt, and it can take responsibility for what it finds.
> 
> The obvious objection: models will get better at ranking. I think they will. But people aren't frozen while that happens. Comparing 2026 Yash with 2027 Claude misses that there will also be a 2027 Yash, with another year of experience, using 2027 Claude.
> 
> As models improve and spread, they raise the floor for everyone. The default answer becomes the baseline. The value is in using your perspective and responsibility to push past it.
> 
> That's why I think we need to change how we measure AI. Instead of only asking how well a model performs on problems everyone agrees are worth solving, ask: How good is it at doing my work, from my perspective?
> 
> The aggregate can show us the capability curve. The intimate helps us see what we might build, and who we might become, as we ride it.
